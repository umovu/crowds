Abstract
The influence of reinforcement on classroom management and learner attainment in secondary schools is one of the key components of a conducive teaching–learning environment. Thus, a conducive learning environment is only possible if a teacher is skilled and able to apply different techniques to ensure the required classroom environment. Consequently, this research aimed to identify the influence of reinforcement on effective classroom management and learners’ attainment. The research employed a generic qualitative method, and a total of 12 purposely sampled teachers and school heads from six secondary schools were interviewed face-to-face. The study found that positive reinforcement does play a role in classroom management and learners’ performance, but that this does not apply in all situations, particularly for learners who come from socioeconomically underprivileged and broken families. However, most teachers did not use positive reinforcement; rather, they used negative reinforcement or punishment to obtain results, contradicting the South African Constitution and the Schools’ Act of 1996 that preserve the rights of a child and prohibit corporal punishment. The study therefore recommends that teachers be given mandatory training in necessary classroom management skills and their school’s code of conduct to maintain discipline in the classroom for the attainment of educational goals.
Introduction
Reinforcement and improvement in the classroom can increase learners’ chances of focusing and engagement in learning (
Khanum et al., 2025
; 
Munna and Kalam, 2021
; 
Onyekwelu, 2025
). 
Khanum et al. (2025)
 and 
Rumfola, 2017
 noted that teachers facilitate positive reinforcement by emphasising learners’ talents, aptitudes, interests, capabilities and personality traits. This helps teachers connect and build close relationships with learners, which facilitates effective communication and development. An effective teacher provides learners with a classroom environment that offers a sense of respect and freedom, care and an appreciation of learners so that the teaching–learning process can happen effectively and its purpose can be achieved (
Salendab and Akmad, 2023
; 
Salendab and Laguda, 2023
).
Classroom management and learners’ attainment are challenges that teachers face during the teaching–learning process. Effective classroom management and teaching–learning methods vary and require different techniques to be effective. A teacher can exercise reinforcement (positive or negative) as one of the techniques for effective classroom management and learners’ academic attainment. It helps teachers advance learners’ performance and hold their attention through educational activities and discussions (
Khanum et al., 2025
; 
Onyekwelu, 2025
; 
Gunaretnam, 2021
; 
Onyekwelu, 2025
; 
Munna and Kalam, 2021
).
Michael, 1975
 introduced reinforcement terminologies and provided four main types of reinforcement: i) “positive reinforcement, ii) negative reinforcement, iii) tangible reinforcement, iv) social reinforcement.” Positive reinforcement is the presentation of a pleasurable or desirable consequence for behaviour, such as praising a student for satisfactory work. Negative reinforcement is removing an unpleasant consequence to increase behaviour, such as restoring recess privilege for assignment completion (
Martin and Sass, 2010
). Tangible reinforcement is a reinforcement that the student can actually touch, such as an award sticker or edibles like gum or candy. Social reinforcement is the feedback we get from other people in response to what we do. It might take the form of smiles, acceptance, praise, applause, and attention. 
Onyekwelu (2025)
, 
Munna and Kalam (2021)
 and 
Owusu et al. (2021)
 found that the effect of reinforcement in the classroom is significant and linked with learners’ attainment. Positive reinforcement has an important role in learners’ behaviours and academic learning and motivates them to do what is right. 
Fatima et al. (2023)
, 
Khanum et al. (2025)
, 
Lynnette et al. (2021)
, and 
Mondol et al. (2026)
 affirmed that teachers who consistently used positive reinforcement strategies such as praise, rewards, encouragement, recognition, and positive feedback experienced improved learner discipline, classroom participation and better academic outcomes. Learners become more cooperative, respectful, attentive, and motivated during lessons. Studies on positive behavioural support indicate that supportive and proactive approaches contribute to improved learner conduct.
Conversely, 
Mondol et al. (2026)
 and 
Munna and Kalam (2021)
 stated that punishment and negative reinforcement are not solutions to the problem. 
Skinner (1963)
 differentiates between punishment and negative reinforcement. He believes that punishment and negative reinforcement increase hostility, anger, and aggression in learners (
Marinda and Suhardi, 2021
; 
Papageorgi, 2021
). Learners demonstrate this aggression as a technique in dealing with classroom activities. It leads them to exhibit negative and unwanted behaviours and creates fear in them from the teacher as well as from the school and classroom. Furthermore, punishment and negative reinforcement cannot create the required behaviours in learners. Negative reinforcement motivates behaviours that are not repeated for a certain time, while punishment only stops them from doing what is wrong and creates negative behaviours (
Marinda and Suhardi, 2021
; 
Zahid and Cheema, 2023
). 
Skinner (1963)
 moreover states that negative behaviours are challenging and cannot be forgotten. These behaviours are suppressed by punishment for a time but resume after a while.
Classroom management is crucial for effective teaching and learners’ academic attainment. Although classroom management and learners’ performance are distinct outcome variables, existing literature reveals a strong relationship between the two. Classroom management refers to the strategies, practices, and behaviours teachers use to create and maintain an orderly, disciplined, and supportive learning environment. Learners’ academic performance, on the other hand, relates to the achievement of educational outcomes such as test scores, class participation, task completion, and overall academic success. The connection between these variables lies in the fact that effective classroom management creates conditions that are conducive to teaching and learning. When classrooms are well managed, disruptions are minimised, learner engagement increases, instructional time is protected, and positive teacher–learner relationships are promoted. These factors collectively enhance learners’ concentration, motivation, and participation, which ultimately contribute to improved academic attainment.
Many researchers (
Aikomo, 2025
; 
Ajani, 2024
) have consistently found that poorly managed classrooms are associated with learner misconduct, absenteeism, low motivation, and reduced academic achievement, whereas positive and structured classroom environments are linked to higher learner performance and improved educational outcomes. In the South African context, the abolition of corporal punishment has further intensified the need for effective alternative classroom management strategies, as many teachers experience challenges in maintaining discipline and learner engagement. Consequently, reinforcement strategies such as praise, rewards, encouragement, and positive behavioural support have become important tools for promoting both classroom order and academic success. Therefore, the present study considers classroom management and learners’ performance as interconnected variables, since the effectiveness of reinforcement strategies in managing learner behaviour may directly influence the quality of teaching and learning and ultimately learners’ academic attainment.
The available literature on teachers’ use of reinforcement for effective classroom management and learners’ performance in South African secondary schools shows the research gaps that need to be addressed. In the context of South Africa, classroom management research has mainly focused on challenges such as behavioural issues, violence, learners’ misconduct, disciplinary policies and the abolishment of corporal punishment, with limited emphasis on reinforcement strategies as a preventative measure for effective classroom management and learners’ academic attainment. Positive reinforcement, such as praise, rewards and incentives, is discussed thoroughly, showing that it has a positive impact on learners’ performance and behaviour modification; however, not much literature explores the application of positive reinforcement strategies in schools. Furthermore, there is a conceptual and contextual gap in understanding how negative reinforcements and punishment relate to the challenges South African schools face. The socioeconomic backgrounds of the learners, poverty, drug abuse, teenage pregnancies, bullying, and disruptive behaviours are some of the concerns of the teachers in classrooms (
Ahmed, 2025
; 
Ahmed, 2024
; 
Ajani, 2024
; 
Du Plessis and Mestry, 2019
; 
Hollins, 2015
; 
Ng et al., 2018
; 
Smith et al., 2022
). 
Ladson-Billings (2014
, p.76) used the terms “culturally relevant pedagogy”, “culturally deprived”, and “culturally disadvantaged” for the learners who belong to this background. Thus, this qualitative research sought to examine the application and the influence of reinforcement on classroom management and learners’ performance. Thus, the research aimed to contribute to the literature by providing empirical evidence on how reinforcement application and practice influence classroom management and learners’ performance in South African secondary school settings.
Aims and objectives
The research aimed to examine the influence of reinforcement on classroom management and learners’ performance at secondary schools by answering the following research questions:

Is reinforcement viewed as having an impact on learners’ attainment?
Is reinforcement viewed as playing a role in classroom management for the effective teaching-learning process?
Theoretical framework
The research employed 
Deci et al., 1989
 Self-Determination Theory (SDT) as its theoretical framework. The SDT focuses on the influence of the social environment on an individual’s attitude, emotions, values, and behaviour. The theory examines human behaviours and believes that “behaviours evolved to be inherently active, intrinsically motivated, and oriented toward developing naturally through integrative processes” (
Deci et al., 1989
, p, 629). In this study, SDT was used to study the effects of “extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation.” According to 
Deci (1971)
, rewards do not always motivate the intrinsic motivation of an individual. He considered intrinsic motivation an “inherent characteristic” and saw it as an example of self-determination of an individual. He further elaborated that rewards could damage or enhance behaviours depending on the need and social environment in which the event happened. If the rewards or “threats of punishment” (
Deci and Cascio, 1972
), “positive feedback” (
Deci, 1971
), “competition” (
Deci et al., 1981
), or “choice” (
Zuckerman et al., 1978
) were anticipated to spoil these needs, it means that the behaviour is controlled by external forces, and demoralises the internal motivation. However, if the event was anticipated to support this, it is envisaged that behaviours are controlled by internal forces that enhance intrinsic motivation. 
Deci et al. (1981)
 and 
Deci et al. (1989)
 further affirmed that financial rewards, competition, threats, and fines spoil individual behaviours and weaken intrinsic motivation. In contrast, affirmative responses, positive feedback, and freedom of choice are motivating factors that enhance confidence, internal motivation, and self-determination (
Deci et al., 1989
; 
Zuckerman et al., 1978
).
Literature review
The law of “effect reinforcement” is a term introduced by 
Skinner (1963)
 and 
Dingliang (2025)
 that states that reinforced behaviours tend to repeat (i.e., strengthen); however, a behaviour that is not reinforced has a propensity to die out (i.e., weaken). 
Skinner (1963)
, 
Estes (1972)
, 
Hineline and Rosales-Ruiz (2013)
 and 
Malott (2021)
 believe that three reactions can change the tendency of behaviours: i) Neutral response, ii) reinforcers, and iii) punishers. Neutral responses: A response of an environment that does not affect positively or negatively the prospects of any behaviour. Reinforcers: Reinforcers are the environmental responses that can be positive or negative in nature andincrease or decrease the chances of behaviour repetition. Rewards, prizes, praise, and merits are the positive reinforcers that increase the probability of behaviour repetition (
Skinner, 1963
; 
Estes, 1972
; 
Hineline and Rosales-Ruiz, 2013
; 
Malott, 2021
). However, 
 Deci et al. (198 9)
 believe that rewards, prizes and merits spoil learners, and they only repeat behaviours for rewards, not for intrinsic motivation and change. Punishers: punishment is an environmental response that lessens the chances of a behaviour being repeated. According to him, it is the “opposite of reinforcement” that deteriorates or minimises positive behaviour. A child can be punished in many ways, for example, direct punishment by reducing break-time or calling the learner into school early in the morning and leaving from school late in the afternoon in order to address unwanted behaviour (
Skinner, 1963
; 
Estes, 1972
; 
Hineline and Rosales-Ruiz, 2013
). However, corporal punishment was abolished in the South African Constitution in 1996. Corporal punishment is therefore banned in schools.
Positive reinforcement in classroom management and learner performance
The term “positive reinforcement” was first pioneered by 
Skinner (1963)
 in his “operant conditioning” theory. 
Skinner (1963)
 stated that if a child or learner was rewarded for their positive and good behaviour, it creates positivity in the child or learner. 
Skinner (1963)
 considered that the reward that was given to a child is a motivation for them to act positively, and this behaviour's repetition is expected from a child after a reward. These rewards and reinforcements encourage learners to behave positively and repeat positive behaviour time and again (
Eremie and Doueyi-Fiderikumo, 2019
; 
Hineline and Rosales-Ruiz, 2013
; 
Marinda and Suhardi, 2021
).
The presentation of a stimulus for the performance of desirable behaviour is referred to as positive reinforcement. The use of desirable stimuli for performance helps reinforce positive behaviour and it is expected to happen repeatedly in the future (
Boakes, 2021
). According to 
Staddon and Cerutti (2003)
, the behaviour theory by 
Skinner (1963)
 is based on consequences. 
Skinner (1963)
 theory states that if a person's behaviour is rewarded with reinforcement or positive consequences, there is a greater chance of this behaviour repeating. On the other hand, he further stated that if a person's behaviour was punished (negative consequences), there are fewer chances of this behaviour occurring again. 
Skinner (1963)
 and 
Staddon and Cerutti (2003)
 emphasise that external factors and motivations are more important than internal factors and motivation for a desired behaviour. 
Skinner (1963)
, 
Staddo and Cerutti (2003)
 further stated that both reinforcements, positive and negative, serve the same purpose, and the purpose is to encourage an individual toward positive behaviour. Positive reinforcement enhances and develops required stimulations in an individual; however, negative reinforcement or punishment eliminates unnecessary behaviour or behaviour that is not required. When a teacher gives a merit to a learner on the completion of homework and withholds break time from a learner who did not complete homework, this is both positive and negative reinforcement, desired to increase required behaviours. Different types of reinforcements can be used to develop learners’ emotional intelligence and required characteristics in behaviour (
Hidayat, 2018
; 
Khilmiyah and Wiyono, 2021
; 
Pittman et al., 2022
).
“Natural reinforcers, social reinforcers, tangible reinforcers, and token reinforcers” are the four main categories of reinforcements that can be used to bring positive change in behaviours (
Hidayat, 2018
; 
Khilmiyah and Wiyono, 2021
). The reinforcers that happen directly are known as natural reinforcers. For example, the learners who work hard in class and pay attention to their studies usually get good marks in their examinations; these good marks are the natural reinforcers for them. The social reinforcers are the reinforcers a teacher uses for a learner in class, in the form of saying excellent work done or by written remarks. Tangible reinforcers refer to a tangible reward, such as a candy, a sticker, or any other tangible thing as a reward. Token reinforcers are the points, rewards or merits that can be replaced or exchanged in the form of valuable things or certificates, etc (
Bhuvaneswari and Baskaran, 2020
.; 
Khilmiyah and Wiyono, 2021
; 
Putri et al., 2024
).
Reinforcement and learners’ performance
In an educational environment, there are several studies on reinforcement and its influence on learners’ performance and behaviour change. 
Bonghawan and Macalisang, (2024)
 stated that positive reinforcement has a significant impact on learners’ performance. 
Bashir et al. (2020)
 and 
Sugai and Horner (2002)
 researched diverse socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds of learners and found that reinforcement affects their behaviour and performance. They further stated that some learners do not change behaviour without reinforcement. Positive reinforcement is one of the best strategies to promote positive changes in behaviour and boost learners’ academic performance. Another study on the effectiveness of positive reinforcement conducted by 
Mirza and Rashid (2024)
, 
Mallillin (2025)
 and 
Orji et al. (2024)
 stated that rewards and punishments are effective tools that can be used for effective classroom management and desired learners’ behaviour and educational outcomes. Learners’ engagement with academics and positive behaviour are the grassroots predictors and the primary goals of the classroom and teaching–learning process. To achieve the objectives of an effective classroom and teaching–learning process, a teacher needs to use reinforcers (positive or negative) when required.
Reinforcement and classroom management
Classroom management is vital for effective teaching and learning, positive behaviour, learners’ academic performance, and the accomplishment of classroom goals and objectives. Effective classroom management is an issue not only in South African schools but a concern of many countries of the world. Some African and Asian countries such as Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Botswana, China, Japan, India and Pakistan, are facing classroom management challenges and attainment of required educational goals (
Egwuasi et al., 2018
; 
Muchena and Moalisi, 2018
). The application of reinforcement is a useful technique that can be practised to attain desired goals. 
Zahid and Cheema (2023)
 and 
Khanum et al. (2025)
 stated that, instead of employing punishment or negative reinforcement for desired educational outcomes, it is always recommended to use positive reinforcement. Positive reinforcement gives motivation and encouragement to learners to repeat the behaviour that is required. 
Khanum et al. (2025)
 also believe that positive reinforcement is useful techniques that can bring positive change in behaviours and promote positivity in learners. This technique is also useful for behaviour modification and behaviour management.
Legal and rights-based acknowledgement
Corporal punishment was banned in South African schools by following the international legal frameworks’ footsteps (
Hunter and Morrell, 2021
). Section 10 of the South African school Act of 1996, and international frameworks such as the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) clearly states that corporal punishment is considered as criminal activity if it is administered by a teacher (
Doughty-Wagner, 2025
; 
Hunter, and Morrell, 2021
). The section 28 of the Constitution of 1996 of the Republic of South Africa guarantees every child's rights and protects them from maltreatment, abuse, degradation, and neglect. Moreover, South Africa is also a signatory to the UNCRC, which demands from the states to protect children rights and guard them from all forms of physical, emotional, psychological and mental abuse. Likewise, the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child also emphasise on child's dignity and well-being.
Research (
Maphosa and Shumba, 2010
) indicates that a teacher can maintain discipline and manage behaviour in classrooms by operating within the framework of the constitution of the country and the school act. Harmful corporal punishment for learner misconduct, such as hitting, slapping, canning, pushing and forcing, is not a solution to the problem (
Obadire and Sinthumule, 2021
). 
Ladan and Yabo (2023)
 stated that this can help in stopping undesirable behaviour for the time being on one hand, but at the same time, on the other hand, it also creates fear, pain and aggression, reduces self-esteem, damages teacher–learner relationships and fails to promote self-confidence and discipline. However, constructive disciplinary consequences such as restorative practices, logical consequences, detention, and withdrawal of privileges are considered useful techniques that help learners to bring positivity to their behaviours (
Bachtiar, 2025
; 
Hulvershorn and Mulholland, 2018
). This can also promote self-confidence and self-respect in learners, build teacher-learner relationship, and encourage learners to maintain safe and supportive learning classroom environment. It is important for a teacher to exercise constructive disciplinary measure in line with school policies and code of conduct. Punishment in any form is banned and considered as criminal act if exercised by a teacher.
Research methods
The researcher employed the generic qualitative research approach to investigate the phenomenon of teacher efficacy and classroom management (
Creswell and Poth, 2018
; 
Neuman, 2014
). Generic qualitative research seeks to “to discover and understand a phenomenon, a process, or the perspectives and worldviews of the people involved” (
Merriam, 2002
). In this research, the researcher sought to comprehend the perspectives and worldviews of the teachers regarding the influence of teacher efficacy on effective classroom management in selected secondary schools in Johannesburg. The data was collected by conducting individual face-to-face interviews with each of the selected secondary school teachers (
Merriam, 2002
; 
Tracy, 2019
). The researcher used the purposive sampling technique to select the schools, school heads and teachers as research participants. A total of 18 teachers and school heads from six secondary schools were interviewed, of whom 14 were female and four were male. Eleven of the participants were aged below 40 and seven were between 40 and 60 years. All the participants held permanent positions at their respective schools. Semi-structured open-ended questions were posed to explore the views, thoughts and experiences of the teachers. All interviews were recorded on a cell phone voice recorder to capture the original voices and words of the participants. Member checks were carried out in order to verify the contributions of each participant. The validity of the research was promoted by cross-checking the findings with the raw data (
Guba and Lincoln, 1989
; 
Lincoln and Guba, 1985
; 
Merriam, 2002
). The Tesch's qualitative data analysis method technique (cited in 
Creswell, 2009
; 
Creswell and Poth, 2016
) was carried out through a systematic process to undertake the research. This method involves topic identification and coding, concept development and categorisation, themes and sub-themes formation and finally the write-up (
Creswell, 2009
). Subsequent to research interviews, the researcher transcribed word to word all audio-recorded interviews and thereafter read through several times to gain a general understanding of the participants’ voices, responses and experiences. Afterwards, similar voices, statements, responses and experiences were grouped and assigned codes to all of them. Consequently, interconnected codes were clustered together so that categories and concepts could be formed. The categories and concepts represented are the persistent responses of the research participants. Themes were then developed when several categories reflected a common meaning related to the research questions and objectives. The codes such as “discipline challenge, behaviour management, misconduct, abolishment of corporal punishment” were grouped together to form a theme “Abolishment of corporal punishment and classroom management”. Other themes were also formed with a similar process. The data was analysed by one rater/analyst, so theme formation did not contradict with disagreement. Informed consent from all participants was obtained before interviews (
Braun and Clarke, 2006
; 
Creswell, 2011
; 
Creswell and Poth, 2016
). The participants’ and schools’ anonymity and confidentiality were ensured through the use of pseudonyms.
Results
The following three themes emerged from the analysis and were interpreted by integrating the theoretical framework and the literature.
Theme 1: abolishment of corporal punishment and classroom management
Corporal punishment was an accepted norm in South African schools before the Bill of Rights of 1996. Since the abolishment of corporal punishment in schools, it has become essential for teachers to learn classroom and behaviour management skills in order to maintain discipline and provide a conducive learning environment for learners. The Bill of Rights protects the educational and social rights of a child and states that teachers are not allowed to discipline learners and manage the class by applying any kind of corporal punishment; i.e., physical, emotional or psychological. The behaviours can only be dealt with reinforcement, rules and rewards. During research, it was found that many teachers struggle to control misbehaviours and are not trained enough to use reinforcements, rewards and merits, etc., as discipline and classroom management strategies. One of the research respondents articulated that “
we did not receive any kind of training to deal with misbehaved and problematic learners during B.Ed. degree.”
 Another respondent added that “
behaviour management training in South African context is missing in B.Ed. degree programme.”
 There is a need to include behaviour management theories and training programmes that align with the South African school context in trainee teachers’ courses so that they can equip themselves to address different learner behaviours.
Corporal punishment is banned, so teachers cannot use it as a strategy to discipline learners, which 
Mahlangu et al. (2021)
 think that it was the right decision, as it has protected the rights of the child, but at the same time, it has also raised issues for teachers to discipline misbehaved and disruptive learners in the classroom. Before corporal punishment was banned, teachers used to exercise physical punishment in schools to manage behaviour. This technique was considered an appropriate solution to the problem. However, 
Aravantinos et al. (2024)
 argued that this was a temporary, not a permanent, solution. Behaviours cannot be modified with punishment, force or authority. They demand attention and relationship, and more importantly, the root causes of misbehaviours to be addressed. The research respondents were of the view that punishment should be allowed in schools at some level to discipline various behaviours. Different behaviours demand different modification techniques. Additionally, another respondent expressed her views that “
I think punishment is to minimise misbehaviours; it does not mean physical punishment. It can be a question asking, or ask the learner to stand-up in classroom, or stop their breaks, etc.”
 After the abolishment of corporal punishment, classroom management is a challenge for both in-training and in-service teachers. Some disruptive learners disrupt the classroom environment by knowing their constitutional rights. It is therefore essential for the teachers to train them in a way so that they can be better able to deal with disruption and misbehaviour.
Theme 2: school code of conduct and classroom management
South African Schools Act (84 of 1996) has set the code of conduct to be exercised for classroom and discipline management in schools. The school governing body is accountable for the adoption of the code of conduct; however, teachers and school management are responsible for its implementation. 
Zondo and Mncube (2024)
 stated that the implementation of the code of conduct has minimised disciplinary issues and enhanced the learning environment. In response to the role of the code of conduct in classroom management, it was identified that it is about the implementation and execution of it for behaviour management and an effective teaching-learning process. Some respondents think that the implementation of class rules and code of conduct is a useful strategy to apply for classroom and behaviour management. Others believe that it is a useful technique, but it does not work in all situations, and on all behaviour. In order to attain educational goals, it is essential to deal with misbehaviours seriously, take disciplinary measures, let learners know about the rules, regulations and code of conduct of the school, and implement them if and where necessary. Moreover, findings also confirmed that codes of conduct are to deal with minor issues and behaviours, but teachers do not know or are not trained to deal with serious behaviours and challenges in the classroom. An argument came up in response to deal with rough behaviours. The respondent said, “
The challenge that I faced in my class was the challenge of drug abuse. Some learners were using XINEX (it's one of the kinds of drugs). After they take XINEX, they become violent, they fight, do not follow rules, and interrupt other learners. I don’t know which code of conduct can be implemented in this situation.”
 The respondent verdict supported the argument of 
Zondo and Mncube (2022)
, who stated that in South Africa, especially in rural schools, teachers are facing multiple challenges in implementing the code of conduct to manage learners’ behaviour. They further stated that some behaviours are unjustifiable and cannot be dealt with or fall under the code of conduct category. Teachers are not fully exposed to the code of conduct, as well as not expert in classroom management skills.
Theme 3: use of reinforcement and classroom management
Positive reinforcement is considered one of the useful strategies for classroom and behaviour management, especially for beginning-grade learners. 
Skinner (1963)
 stated that reinforcement can be exercised either positively or negatively for classroom and behaviour management. Positive reinforcements such as rewards and prizes can motivate one to repeat the same behaviour for reward. Similar to positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement, such as a fine or unpleasant activity assigned by a teacher to a learner as punishment, can enhance the chances of that action not being repeated. The research respondent stated that “
acknowledgement as a positive reinforcement can play a positive role in effective classroom management.”
 The findings conclude that reinforcement is a technique that teachers employ to achieve educational goals and classroom management. It was also found that rewards and reinforcers do impact ill-behaviours, but only for a specific time. After an interval, the same behaviour repetition occurred. This hinders effective classroom management and teaching-learning activities. Although punishment is not allowed, some teachers still believe that this can be a solution. On the other hand, 
Glasser (1999)
 affirmed that any type of negative reinforcement cannot bring positivity in learners’ behaviour. Behaviour cannot be modified with punishment or hard and strict rules, but managed with positivity. One respondent said, 
“In my view, we cannot manage classroom with punishment or fines. Reinforcement as a punishment is not a solution, rather we can use it positively by praising or giving them rewards on any minor achievements.”
 This argument is also supported by Plato as cited by 
Chandra, 2015
: “Do not train learners to learning by force and harshness but direct them to it by what amuses their mind, so that you may be better able to discover with accuracy the peculiar bent of the genius of each”. 
Glasser (1999)
 also affirmed that punishment cannot motivate one to bring positive change in behaviour. It is a challenging situation for teachers in schools, they cannot punish learners to keep them on track, and they are also not trained enough to implement reinforcement (positive and negative) and use the code of conduct for behaviour management. One more respondent argued that “
we need to create a balance. Learners in all schools are not same and we cannot deal with all of them in a same way.”
 A balance theory in B.Ed. coursework and practical training is crucial to deal with the challenge.
Discussion
Teachers’ use of reinforcement in the classroom is a great concern for many researchers and teachers. The term reinforcement was first introduced by 
Skinner (1963)
. He believes that reinforced behaviours have the tendency to be repeated, while a behaviour that is not reinforced has the chance to remain constant. Multiple studies (
Aravantinos et al. 2024
) confirm that reinforcements cannot bring positive change in behaviour. 
Skinner (1963)
; 
Estes (1972)
; 
Hineline and Rosales-Ruiz (2013)
 and 
Malott (2021)
 argued that rewards, prizes, praise, and merits are the positive reinforcers that increase the probability of behaviour repetition, while 
Deci et al. (1989)
 viewed rewards, prizes and merits as spoiling learners. Reinforced behaviours are repeated for rewards only, not for intrinsic motivation and change
.
In an endeavour to investigate teachers’ practices on the use of reinforcement in classrooms to attain learners’ performance and classroom management for effective teaching and learning, it was found that positive reinforcement, such as rewards, recognition, praise and merits, can be a good strategy to use in the classroom to manage behaviour. The research also found that positive reinforcement contributes to learners’ academic attainment. Learners who received encouragement and recognition were more likely to participate in lessons, complete academic activities successfully and achieve better educational outcomes. However, this might bring a transitory change in behaviour. The positive behaviours tend to be repeated only when there is appreciation, encouragement, rewards, acknowledgement, and praise. Moreover, neither negative reinforcement such as extra work and extra lessons after school, nor punishment, such as detention, removing break time, stopping access to cell phones and physical punishment, is workable in every situation and for all behaviours. Negative reinforcement increases positive behaviour by eliminating undesirable events and works as a positive approach to on-track behaviour. In contrast, punishment brings negativity and can be demoralising; it cannot be a source of encouragement, motivation and positivity. Thus, the effects of punishment on learners are typically undesirable and can leave learners with unpleasant memories.
The research findings align with 
Hineline and Rosales-Ruiz, 2013
 and 
Malott (2021)
 view that negative reinforcement and punishment are not permanent solutions to the problem. Moreover, the findings indicate that it is difficult to motivate learners from economically underprivileged and broken families with incentives and rewards. They usually face difficult times and challenges, and it is expected that they are more likely to misbehave socially and emotionally in the classroom. For example, some participants noted that learners from poor economic backgrounds and broken families have more social issues, and it is hard to deal with these behaviours with reinforcement or punishment. Behaviour modification and change require a healthy, positive and comfortable environment (
Antoniuk et al. 2023
; 
Gardner, 2021
), and it is difficult for these learners to access such environments. However, while this might be true, it cannot be generalised, as some learners from underprivileged and single-headed families are well-behaved and perform excellently in class.
Maintaining discipline in South African classrooms, like many countries such as Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Botswana, China, Japan, India and Pakistan, is a challenge that most teachers face today (
Obadire and Sinthumule, 2021
; 
Segalo and Rambuda, 2018
). Teachers use different techniques to manage behaviour and maintain discipline in classrooms. Corporal punishment is against educational law. However, reinforcements in this study's context were not seen as a solution to the problem. Permanent solutions were needed, but the participants did not know what these could be, as even applying the school code of conduct, rules and restorative measures have proven not to be permanent solutions in all situations and contexts. The research findings also revealed the challenges associated with the implementation of reinforcement strategies. Some respondents reported difficulties in maintaining discipline without corporal punishment, especially in overcrowded classrooms and under-resourced schools. Others indicated that teachers are not skilled in implementing reinforcement techniques. Moreover, they also lacked classroom management training. After the abolishment of corporal punishment, teachers further struggle to balance learner rights with effective discipline practices and the application of reinforcement strategies. By knowing that physical punishment is banned, some teachers in rural and non-fee schools still use it; however, research has shown that negative reinforcement and punishment do not bring positivity and behaviour change, and positive reinforcement can mean learners only repeat the behaviour for incentives. The literature shows that rewards do not always motivate intrinsic motivation. Intrinsic motivation is an “inherent characteristic” that is the self-determination of an individual (
Tamir and Hughes, 2018
; 
Schultner et al., 2025
). 
Tamir and Hughes (2018)
 and 
Schultner et al. (2025)
 further believe that rewards could damage or enhance behaviours depending on the needs and social environment of the classroom. This is a frustrating dichotomy as reinforcement does not always work, and punishment is not allowed.
Even so, in order to provide a conducive classroom, the participants felt that, in some situations, teachers should use negative reinforcement (restorative practices, demerits, etc.) when needed. The learners should not be given more rights than teachers because of their different socioeconomic, cultural and family backgrounds, and the school environment and strategies and rules should be context-specific. All behaviours and backgrounds cannot be managed in the same way. 
Tesfay and Elias (2025)
 affirmed that teacher interventions in line with the law are significant if class rules are broken and learners are misbehaving. Furthermore, 
Glasser (1999)
 affirmed the same in his “choice theory”, that behaviour modification and learners’ performance can be achieved through positive reinforcement, but not through punishment, harshness, power and authority. Similarly, 
Kohn, 2006
, 
Kubanek et al. (2015)
 and 
Skinner (1963)
 argued that punishment and negative reinforcement are not the solution to behaviour modification and problems. Conversely, most participants felt that reinforcement rewards and punishments cannot be set aside in all cases, which is not justifiable according to South African law.
The research findings signify that for effective classroom management and educational outcomes, schools and teachers must implement positive reinforcement and a school code of conduct to maintain discipline in the classroom. Furthermore, corporal punishment is abolished, and much research, such as this study, has found that any type of reinforcement (positive, negative and punishment) is not a permanent solution. Therefore, it is vital that teachers develop the necessary classroom management skills.
While reinforcement and punishment are not solutions to the problem, teachers need to be skilled so that they can cultivate positive and close relationships with learners that can build confidence and trust between them. Teachers can use this bond to deal with negative behaviours. Moreover, modern technological interventions in classrooms and diverse and challenging learners’ backgrounds demand context-specific professional training programmes to train teachers according to the needs and demands of the learners.
Conclusion
In conclusion, the research demonstrates that classroom management and learners’ academic attainment are problems in some secondary schools in Johannesburg. The findings suggest that although positive reinforcement, such as praise, constructive feedback and rewards, contributes to effective teaching and learning, an improvement in learner behaviour, an increase in motivation and enhancement in learners’ academic attainment and effective classroom management, this is not a solution to the problem. This creates habits that are problematic for learners, as they respond and act positively only when behaviour is dealt with through positive reinforcement. Furthermore, the research highlights that some schools, such as quintile 1 and quintile 2 (no-fee) schools, which are mostly in remote areas, have more classroom and learner academic attainment problems than quintile 3 and Model C schools (fee-paying). Underprivileged socioeconomic and broken family backgrounds are the main reasons for this. Learners with these backgrounds are often difficult to handle with rewards, praise and positive feedback. Corporal punishment and the use of power and authority are prohibited according to the law, so they cannot be used to control behaviour. Negative reinforcement has its own challenges, as these are less effective for long-term change. In addition, teachers lack the skills to manage classrooms effectively by implementing other strategies. Therefore, the research underscores the need for a shift from the use of reinforcement and punishment to teachers’ professional development so that they can learn, improve and implement different strategies for effective classroom environments without infringing the rights of the child.
The study had some limitations as it was conducted in six secondary schools of Johannesburg, which is limited in size. However, it is expected that many other South African secondary schools find themselves in the same scenario. The research only focused on the influence of reinforcements on classroom management and learners’ academic attainment, and did not discuss other factors that influence classroom management and learner performance. The findings of this qualitative study are context-specific. The research topic, participants’ views, thoughts and experiences, societal challenges and learners’ background are some of the important topics that need to be discussed further and in detail with a larger, diverse sample.
